Part0 Calibrating the Camera

Part0.1 Calibrating Your Camera

Pipeline:

  1. Loop through all the calibration images
  2. For each image, detect the ArUco tags using OpenCV's ArUco detector
  3. Extract the corner coordinates from the detected tags
  4. Collect all detected corners and their corresponding 3D world coordinates (e.g., my tag is 0.6m × 0.6m, so the corners should be \([(0,0,0), (0.6,0,0), (0.6,0.6,0), (0,0.6,0)]\)
  5. Use cv2.calibrateCamera() to compute the camera intrinsics (\(K\)) and distortion coefficients

\[ \mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

where \((f_x, f_y)\) are the focal lengths and \((o_x = image\_width/2,\; o_y = image\_height/2)\) are the principal points

Why use ArUco Tags:

OpenCV provides reliable detectors for ArUco markers, allowing us to automatically obtain the 2D pixel coordinates (u,v) of each marker's corners. Since the real-world size of each tag is known, we can assign consistent 3D world coordinates to these corners. With the 2D–3D correspondences from multiple images, we can run camera calibration (solvePnP + calibration routines) to estimate the camera's intrinsic parameters and poses.

Part0.2 Capturing a 3D Object Scan

Why need to use the same camera and zoom level:

Camera calibration estimates the intrinsic parameters of a camera and we want an intrinsic matrix (K) shared across different views. K is consisted of focal lengths (\(fx\), \(fy\)) and principal points (\(ox\), \(oy\)), which vary with the camera and zoom level, so we want these parameters to be the same.

Why use 6 tags rather than a single tag as instructed:

ArUco tags are for detecting (u,v) coordinate (explanation see Part0.1), so the object cannot obscure the tag(s). As we are going to take photos around the object, if there's only one tag, then it will certainly be obscured. So eventually I chose 6 tags.

Part 0.3: Estimating Camera Pose

After calibrating the camera, we can use its intrinsic parameters to determine the pose (orientation and position) for each object image. This reduces to the classic Perspective–n–Point (PnP) problem: given a set of 3D points in the world and their corresponding 2D projections in an image, we estimate the camera’s extrinsic parameters.

For each image in the object scan, we detect the single ArUco marker and use cv2.solvePnP() to recover the camera pose.

1. Inputs to solvePnP:

  • objectPoints: The 3D coordinates of the ArUco marker’s corners in world space. Because the tag’s physical dimensions are known (here we used 0.6m again), these points can be defined explicitly.
  • imagePoints: The detected 2D pixel locations of the tag corners in the image (obtained from detectMarkers()).
  • cameraMatrix: The intrinsic matrix \(K\)
  • distCoeffs: The distortion coefficient vector.

2. Outputs from solvePnP:

  • success (bool): Indicates whether pose estimation was successful.
  • rvec (numpy array of shape (3, 1)): The rotation expressed as a Rodrigues vector (convertible to a \(3 \times 3\) rotation matrix using cv2.Rodrigues()).
  • tvec (numpy array of shape (3, 1)): The translation of the camera expressed in world coordinates.

These outputs define the world-to-camera transformation (\(w2c\)). Since later stages require the camera-to-world matrix (\(c2w\)), we will compute its inverse.

Positional Encoding (Important Trick for Reconstructing Details):

MLPs are biased toward learning very smooth, low-frequency functions (spectral bias). They learn low-frequency patterns easily but struggle with high-frequency signals (sharp edges, textures, glossy highlights). Without positional encoding, the input of the MLPs are low-dimension, leading to blurry output images since the MLPs cannot learn the details well. Positional encoding basically expands the input from low dimensions to high dimensions using the formula below.

$$ PE(x) = \{x, \sin(2^0\pi x), \cos(2^0\pi x), \sin(2^1\pi x), \cos(2^1\pi x), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi x), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi x)\} $$

in which L is the highest frequency level

Max Frequency Parameter (L): 3 or 10
Input dimention:

$$ 2 + 4 \times L \quad (e.g., L = 10 \text{ gives } in\_dim = 42) $$

(Part 2.1) Create Rays from Cameras

1. Camera to World Coordinate Conversion

A point observed in world coordinates \(\mathbf{X}_w = (x_w, y_w, z_w)\) can be mapped into the camera’s coordinate frame \(\mathbf{X}_c = (x_c, y_c, z_c)\) through a rigid transformation consisting of a rotation \(\mathbf{R}_{3\times3}\) and a translation vector \(\mathbf{t}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_c \\ y_c \\ z_c \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}_{3\times3} & \mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0}_{1 \times 3} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_w \\ y_w \\ z_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

This block matrix is commonly referred to as the world-to-camera (\(w2c\)) extrinsic matrix. Taking its inverse gives the camera-to-world (\(c2w\)) transform, which maps points back into world space.

2. Pixel to Camera Coordinate Conversion

In a standard pinhole camera model, the intrinsic matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) encodes the focal lengths \((f_x, f_y)\) as well as the principal point \((o_x = image\_width/2,\; o_y = image\_height/2)\). This matrix takes a point in the camera’s 3D coordinate system and projects it onto the 2D pixel plane:

\[ \mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Using this intrinsic matrix, a 3D camera-space position \((x_c, y_c, z_c)\) is mapped to pixel coordinates \((u, v)\) as follows:

\[ s \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{K} \begin{bmatrix} x_c \\ y_c \\ z_c \end{bmatrix}, \quad\text{where } s = z_c \]

3. Pixel to Ray

A ray can be represented by two components: an origin \(\mathbf{r}_o \in \mathbb{R}^3\) and a direction vector \(\mathbf{r}_d \in \mathbb{R}^3\). For a pinhole camera, we construct one such ray for every pixel \((u, v)\) in the image.

If we denote the camera-to-world matrix as \[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \] then the origin of all rays is simply the translation component:

\[ \mathbf{r}_o = \mathbf{t} \]

To obtain the direction, we take a point along the ray at depth \(s = 1\) in camera coordinates, convert this point to the world frame using the previous transformations, and subtract the ray origin. Finally, we normalize the resulting vector:

\[ \mathbf{r}_d = \frac{ \mathbf{X}_w - \mathbf{r}_o }{ \lVert \mathbf{X}_w - \mathbf{r}_o \rVert_2 } \]

(Part2.2) Sampling

1. Sampling Rays from Images

To sample N rays at every training iteration:

  • Option 1: first sample M images, and then sample N // M rays from every image.
  • Option 2 : flatten all pixels from all images and do a global sampling once to get N rays from all images.

I chose Option 2.

The main idea of sampling rays from images are also illustrated below (give credit to Aqeel Anwar: https://medium.com/data-science/a-12-step-visual-guide-to-understanding-nerf-representing-scenes-as-neural-radiance-fields-24a36aef909a).

2. Sampling Points along Rays

Once we obtain a ray, NeRF evaluates the scene at several points along that ray to approximate the continuous volumetric rendering integral. This requires selecting a sequence of depth values between a near bound and a far bound, then converting each depth value into a 3D coordinate using \(\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{r}_o+t\mathbf{r}_d\). To avoid overfitting to a fixed set of 3D locations, the depth samples are typically perturbed slightly within their intervals during training. These sampled 3D points are then fed into the NeRF model to query density and color, which are later accumulated to compute the pixel’s predicted RGB value.

(for lego.npz) near = 2.0, far = 6.0, n_samples = 64

Again, here's an illustration from Aqeel Anwar. After we get the corresponding ray from a pixel, we sample a specific number of 3D points along the ray between the near plane and the far plane. Then, each 3D coordinate (xyz) as well as the corresponding view direction (2D) would be put into the MLP for training. For a specific ray, the viewing directions for all the points are the same.

(Part2.3) Putting all Dataloading together

I constructed a RaysData class, which is basically the custom dataloader used in NeRF. Its job is to store the multi-view images and their camera poses, convert pixel coordinates into rays, and randomly sample rays during training. For a more detailed walk through, it first converts all inputs to tensors on the same device, then precomputes a full pixel grid for each image and associates every pixel with its camera index. When sample_rays(B) is called, the dataloader uniformly selects B pixels from all cameras, retrieves the corresponding camera-to-world matrices, and converts each pixel into a world-space ray by computing its origin at the camera center and its direction from normalized camera-space coordinates. Eventually, the function returns ray origins, directions, pixel coordinates, and camera IDs.

(Part2.5) Volume Rendering

Volume rendering describes how a ray accumulates color as it travels through a scene. At every tiny step along the ray, the local density \( \sigma \) determines how likely the ray is to "stop" and reveal the color there, while the transmittance \( T(t) \) controls how much light from farther points can still pass through. The continuous formulation integrates these infinitesimal contributions:

\[ C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,c(\mathbf{r}(t),\mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, ds\right) \]

Since pixels are discrete, NeRF uses a discrete approximation. The ray is sampled at \(N\) points, and each point contributes its color depending on (1) the probability the ray has not terminated before that sample (the accumulated transmittance \(T_i\)) and (2) the probability of terminating at that sample. Summing these weighted contributions yields:

\[ \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \left(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)\right)c_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j \right) \]

Here, \(c_i\) is the color predicted at sample \(i\), \(T_i\) is the probability that the ray reaches sample \(i\) without terminating, and \(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\) is the probability of ending at that point.
