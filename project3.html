A.2: Recover Homographies

Algorithms:

We use a homography matrix \(H\) to represent the projective transformation from image 1 to image 2.

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix} $$

\(h_{11}, h_{12}, h_{21}, h_{22}\): Affine Transformation - controls rotation, scaling, and shearing.
\(h_{13}, h_{23}\): Translation
\(h_{31}, h_{32}\): Perspective/Projective - Introduces perspective distortion; makes parallel lines converge.
\(h_{33}\): Normalization - Usually set to 1 for scale normalization; no geometric meaning.

For an arbitrary point \((x, y)\) in image 1, represented as \(p\) in homogeneous coordinate, and an arbitrary point \((x', y')\) in image 2, represented as \(p'\), \(H\) maps \((x, y)\) to \((x', y')\)

$$ p = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad p' = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad p' \sim Hp = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} \\ h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} \\ h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33} \end{bmatrix} $$

Comparing \(p'\) with \(Hp\), we get:

$$ x' = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}}, \quad y' = \frac{h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}} $$

Expanding the two expressions, we have the following linear equations:

$$ x'(h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}) = h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} $$

$$ y'(h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}) = h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} $$

As is known to all, linear equations can be expressed as matrix:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'x & -x'y & -x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -y'x & -y'y & -y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ h_{13} \\ h_{21} \\ h_{22} \\ h_{23} \\ h_{31} \\ h_{32} \\ h_{33} \end{bmatrix} = 0 $$

Stacking all \(n\) correspondences produces the matrix-vector multiplication \(Ah = 0\), where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{2n\times9}\) and \(h\) is the 9-element vector formed by expanding \(H\).

The unknown is vector \(h\). Solving \(h\) gives us the homography matrix \(H\) between each two images.

A.3: Warp the Images

Here are three sets of warped images:

Algorithm - Inverse Warping:

The homography matrix H we calculated previously is for transforming image 1 (reference) to image 2 (to be warped). Some mapped values may be floating numbers rather than integers. For example, point (1, 3) in image 1 may be (5.7, 9.3) in image 2 after mapping. However, since pixels are discrete, (5.7, 9.3) will be rounded to integers. Thus, some pixels in image 2 may be missed, resulting in holes, which is not what we want.

To address the problem, we do inverse warping, applying the inverse of matrix H to image 2 to correspond with image 1.

However, similar problems emerged. This time, (1, 3) in image 2 may be mapped to (5.7, 9.3) in image 1. Then which pixel value of image 1 should we pick?

Here we adopt two methods -- nearest neighbor interpolation and bilinear interpolation, with their respective advantages and disadvantages.

1. Nearest neighbor interpolation

As the name suggests, we pick the closest neighbor in image 1. Thus, (5.7, 9.3) would be (6, 9) and we take the RGB values at (6, 9) in image 1 to be that of image 2.

2. Bilinear interpolation

As is illustrated in the diagram, each square cell represents four known pixel values at coordinates \((Y, X)\), \((Y, X + 1)\), \((Y + 1, X)\), \((Y + 1, X + 1)\). The offsets \(w = X' - X\) and \(h = Y' - Y\) measure how far the point is from the top-left corner horizontally and vertically. The closer the point is to a corner, the more that corner’s pixel value influences the result. Bilinear interpolation performs two stages of linear blending:

first along the horizontal direction to compute intermediate values on the top and bottom edges,

\( I_{\text{top}} = (1 - w)\, I(Y, X) + w\, I(Y, X + 1) \)

\( I_{\text{bottom}} = (1 - w)\, I(Y + 1, X) + w\, I(Y + 1, X + 1) \)

and then along the vertical direction between those two results:

\( I(Y', X') = (1 - h)\, I_{\text{top}} + h\, I_{\text{bottom}} \)

3. Comparisons

Nearest neighbor interpolation assigns each new pixel the value of the closest existing pixel. This method is computationally fast and preserves hard edges, but it often produces blocky or jagged results because it does not account for gradual intensity changes.

Bilinear interpolation, on the other hand, computes the new pixel value as a weighted average of the four nearest pixels in a 2×2 neighborhood. The weights depend on the pixel’s relative position between them. This process smooths transitions and produces more visually continuous images. However, it also blurs fine details slightly and is slower than nearest neighbor due to the additional arithmetic operations.

In summary, nearest neighbor is fast but rough, while bilinear is smoother but softer. The choice depends on the application: use nearest neighbor for categorical or discrete data, and bilinear when smooth gradients and natural-looking images are more important.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Here are the results:

Step 1: Determine mosaic canvas size

  • For each image, take its width \(w\) and height \(h\) and construct its four corner coordinates.
  • Multiply each corner by its homography \(H\) to get transformed corner positions in the global space.
  • Normalize by dividing x and y by the third coordinate.
  • Track the global min and max of all x and y coordinates across all images.
  • These define the mosaic canvas size \(width = x_{max} - x_{min}, height = y_{max} - y_{min}\)

Step 2: Place two images on the canvas

  • Image 1 (reference) is placed at the left of the canvas, right next to the left edge of the canvas.
  • Image 2 (warped) is shifted according to the coordinates of its selected points after warping.

Step 3: Create alpha masks (weight maps)

  • Compute a distance transform of the mask using distance_transform_edt
  • Normalize the distance values to [0, 1]: Pixels far from edges → \(alpha\) ≈ 1; Pixels near edges → \(alpha\) ≈ 0

Step 4: Weighted averaging and final blending

  • Initialize accumulators: \(mosaic_{sum}\) = zeros (same shape as canvas), \(weight_{sum}\) = zeros (2D)
  • Accumulate weighted pixel values: Expand \(alpha\) to match color channels if necessary.
  • Normalize the result: Divide \(mosaic_{sum}\) by \(weight_{sum}\); replace zeros in \(weight_{sum}\) by 1 to avoid division by zero.
  • This blending method smooths transitions and eliminates visible edge artifacts.

Part B: Feature Matching for Autostitching

B.1: Harris Corner Detection

Start with Harris Interest Point Detector (using the provided harris.py)

The algorithm computes the image gradients in both x and y directions, forms the structure tensor \( M = \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \), and calculates the Harris response \( R = \det(M) - k(\operatorname{trace}(M))^2 \), where \( k \) is a small empirical constant. Local maxima of \( R \) correspond to strong corners.

In the original provided harris.py, min_distance between two interest points is 1. However, it seems that the algorithm is capturing too many interest points. I changed the min_distance to be 10 and 100 and the results are as below. To better visualize, min_distance = 100 is good. However, min_distance = 100 turned out to be too strict for later algorithms that there would be too few interest points for matching. Thus, for later implementations, I keep min_distance = 10.

Implement Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS)

Matching features becomes increasingly expensive as the number of detected points grows, we want to limit how many interest points are used from each image. However, simply choosing the strongest responses often leads to many clustered points in textured regions and few elsewhere.

The Harris-only detections include many closely clustered corners, especially in textured regions. Many strong corners might cluster in highly textured areas (like brick walls or foliage), leaving large smooth regions (like sky or ground) with no keypoints at all.

With ANMS, keypoints cover all parts of the image, so every region contributes information to later stages like feature matching or homography estimation, making the distribution of the interest points more spatially balanced.

Algorithms of ANMS:

For each corner \( x_i \) with Harris response \( f(x_i) \), we compute a suppression radius \( r_i \) defined as:

$$ r_i = \min_j \| x_i - x_j \|, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(x_i) < c_{\text{robust}} \, f(x_j), \quad x_j \in \mathcal{I} $$

where \( \mathcal{I} \) is the set of all interest point locations. Intuitively, \( r_i \) measures how far we must go from \( x_i \) before finding a much stronger corner. A large \( r_i \) means the point \( x_i \) is both strong and isolated.

We use \( c_{\text{robust}} = 0.9 \). After computing \( r_i \) for all corners, we sort them by radius in a decreasing order and keep the top \( N = 500 \) points as our final set of ANMS-selected corners.

B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

Around each selected keypoint, extract a 40×40 window from the grayscale image, using a spacing of 5 pixels (experimented by the original paper to be of the best cost-and-benefit). This larger patch was blurred and then subsampled into a smaller 8×8 descriptor to reduce sensitivity to small translations and noise. Each descriptor was then normalized by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation (bias / gain normalization), producing zero-mean, unit-variance feature vectors.

B.3: Feature Matching

Algorithm Steps:

  1. Compute pairwise distances
    For every descriptor in image 1, compute its squared distance to all descriptors in image 2 using the Sum of Squared Differences (SSD):
    \( D(i, j) = \| f_i - f'_j \|_2^2 \)
    This gives a distance matrix showing how similar each pair of features is.
  2. Find the two nearest neighbors
    For each descriptor in image 1, find its closest and second-closest matches in image 2 by sorting the SSD values in ascending order.
  3. Apply Lowe’s ratio test
    Keep the match only if the closest match is much better than the second-closest:
    \( \text{ratio} = \frac{\text{SSD}_{1}}{\text{SSD}_{2}} < \text{ratio_thresh}\)
    The smaller ratio_thresh is, the stricter the selection of interest points would be. We adopt ratio_thresh = 0.7.
  4. Collect and sort matches
    Store all remaining matches as pairs \((i_1, i_2)\) with their SSD scores, then sort them so that the strongest (smallest SSD) come first.

In summary, the feature matching step computes distances between descriptors, keeps only distinct and confident matches using Lowe’s ratio test, and outputs reliable correspondences for RANSAC-based homography estimation.

B.4: RANSAC for Robust Homography

Even after ratio filtering, a few mismatched correspondences can produce inaccurate homographies. To obtain a robust estimate, implement 4-point RANSAC as described below (from a course slide).

In each iteration, randomly select 4 matching pairs, computed a candidate homography \( H \), and counted the number of inliers satisfying \( \|p'_i - H p_i\| < \text{inlier_thresh}\), with \( \text{inlier_thresh} = 3 \) pixels. After N iterations, the \( H \) with the largest inlier count was selected, and a final least-squares fit was recomputed using all inliers.
